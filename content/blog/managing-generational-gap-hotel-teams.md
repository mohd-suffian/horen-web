Walk through a busy hotel and you may find four generations on the same floor: a Gen X general manager, millennial department heads, and Gen Z frontliners in their first or second job. Each group brings strengths. Each also brings assumptions about how work should be done, and those assumptions are where friction starts.

A quick note before we go further: generational labels describe broad tendencies, not individuals. The point is not to stereotype anyone. It is to notice that expectations differ, and that most team conflict comes from expectations nobody has said out loud.

## Where the friction usually shows up

**Communication channels.** Some prefer a quick WhatsApp message. Others expect important things to be said face to face. A shift change announced in a group chat can feel efficient to one person and dismissive to another.

**Feedback.** Newer team members often want frequent, informal feedback. Leaders used to an annual appraisal may only speak up when something goes wrong, which younger staff can read as disapproval.

**Hierarchy.** Some were trained to follow the SOP without question. Others want to know why the SOP exists before they commit to it. Neither is disrespect. They simply learned different rules.

**Career pace.** A two-year wait for promotion may feel normal to one generation and like standing still to another.

## Five things leaders can do this month

**1. Make team norms explicit.** Agree as a team which channel is used for what. Urgent operational changes: in person or a call. Rosters: the group chat. Feedback: one to one. Writing it down removes a surprising amount of tension.

**2. Explain the why.** When you introduce or enforce a standard, give the reason in one sentence. "We confirm the guest's name twice because a wrong room key is a security risk" earns more commitment than "because that's the procedure".

**3. Shorten the feedback loop.** A two-minute check-in at the end of a shift ("one thing that went well, one thing to try tomorrow") works for every generation and costs almost nothing.

**4. Pair across generations.** Reverse mentoring works well in hotels. A younger team member shows a senior colleague a faster way to use the PMS or social media. The senior colleague shares how they handle a VIP or a difficult complaint. Both gain, and respect builds both ways.

**5. Show the path.** Map out what growth looks like in your department, including skills, cross-training and stretch assignments, not only job titles. People stay when they can see where they are going.

## The leadership skill underneath it all

Managing across generations is really about flexing your style without lowering your standards. That takes self-awareness, and it takes practice.
