Every hotel gets complaints. The room is not ready, the air-conditioning is noisy, the breakfast queue is long. What separates a property guests return to from one they review harshly is rarely the absence of problems. It is how the team responds when one happens.

## Why recovery matters

A guest who complains is giving you a second chance. Many unhappy guests never say anything to staff. They simply leave, and some of them say it online instead. The ones who speak up are telling you exactly what went wrong while there is still time to fix it.

Handled well, a complaint can even strengthen the relationship. Guests remember how they were treated when something went wrong far longer than they remember a smooth stay. Handled badly, a small issue becomes the whole story of their visit.

## A simple framework for frontliners

We teach a five-step approach that works at the front desk, in F&B and in housekeeping.

**1. Listen fully.** Let the guest finish. Don't interrupt to explain or defend. Take notes if the issue is detailed, which also shows you are taking it seriously.

**2. Acknowledge the impact.** "I understand. You've had a long journey and the room wasn't ready. That's frustrating." Acknowledging how they feel is not admitting fault. It tells them they've been heard.

**3. Apologise sincerely.** A short, genuine apology beats a long scripted one. "I'm sorry this happened" is enough.

**4. Act, and say what you are doing.** Fix what can be fixed, and give a realistic timeframe. If you need to involve another department, tell the guest who and when.

**5. Follow up.** A call to the room an hour later, or a word at breakfast the next morning, turns a fixed problem into a remembered gesture.

## Empower the people closest to the guest

The biggest barrier to good recovery is often policy, not attitude. If a frontliner has to find a manager before they can offer anything, the guest waits, the frustration grows and the moment passes.

Agree clear limits in advance. For example, frontline staff may offer a complimentary drink, a late check-out or a dessert without approval, while supervisors can authorise a room upgrade. When people know what they are allowed to do, they act quickly and confidently.

## Learn from every complaint

Log complaints in one place and review them weekly. Look for patterns rather than blame. Three complaints about slow room service on Friday nights is not a staff problem. It is a staffing or process problem, and it can be fixed.
